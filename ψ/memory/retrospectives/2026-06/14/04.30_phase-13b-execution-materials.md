## Lessons Learned

**Lesson 1: Embed operational frameworks directly into execution plans, not separately**

The bottleneck prevention framework (1h cadence, proactive escalation, decision gates) could have been a standalone document. Instead, I wove it into EXECUTION-PLAN.md as the monitoring section. This created two benefits: (1) executors see the operational control as integral to success, not a compliance layer; (2) reviewers can't ignore it because it's embedded in the critical path.

**Applied rule**: When a cross-cutting concern (monitoring, escalation, fallback) supports an execution plan, put it in the same document at the relevant section. Separate documents fragment attention and reduce adoption.

**Lesson 2: Gate-driven delivery reduces decision paralysis**

I included 2 hard gates (Thu 15:00 Sprint 1, Wed 18:00 Sprint 2) with clear go/no-go criteria. This meant that instead of Codex-01 asking "when do I know I'm done?", the answer was "when Thu 15:00 arrives and Aeimathes validates, you know." Gates convert subjective completion into objective milestone.

**Applied rule**: For multi-week execution with support teams, define gates with validation criteria upfront. This prevents scope creep (can't negotiate the gate) and reduces back-and-forth (progress is measured against the gate, not against vague expectations).

**Lesson 3: Approval speed is a proxy for framework clarity, not agreement**

User approved Phase 13b in one turn without visible pushback. This could mean the framework was clear, or I over-sold. I didn't verify. Next time: ask one concrete verification question to confirm the user engaged, not just approved.

**Applied rule**: Fast approval is good signal, but only if verified with a concrete follow-up question. Don't assume silence means agreement—it might mean distraction.
